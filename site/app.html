My client was passed over for a dispatch supervisor role at Apex. She's Black, the role went to a white driver with 18 months less experience. Apex says it was about "leadership presence." She has the dispatch logs and a performance review from January that called her "ready for promotion." What does her prima facie case look like in the Fifth Circuit, and what's the strongest defense Apex is going to lead with?
● Analystvia Researcher's pullsjust now

Prima facie strength: solid. In the Fifth Circuit, McDonnell Douglas burden-shifting controls. Your client needs to show (1) membership in a protected class, (2) qualification for the role, (3) an adverse employment action, and (4) circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination — typically that a similarly situated employee outside the protected class was treated more favorably. See ✓McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973) and the Fifth Circuit's application in ✓Davis v. Dallas Area Rapid Transit, 383 F.3d 309 (5th Cir. 2004).

The January performance review calling her "ready for promotion" gets you element (2) without fight. The 18-months-experience gap and the same-job-family comparator gets you element (4) — and is usually enough for the Fifth Circuit to find similarly situated. See ✓Lee v. Kansas City S. Ry., 574 F.3d 253, 260 (5th Cir. 2009) (similarly-situated analysis under "nearly identical" standard).

Apex's likely move: they'll concede the prima facie case (or contest qualification weakly) and lead with "legitimate non-discriminatory reason" at step two — almost certainly the "leadership presence" framing. That's their problem. Subjective criteria like "leadership presence" don't get the same deference as objective metrics, and the Fifth Circuit has repeatedly let plaintiffs reach a jury where the asserted reason is unmoored from documented performance. See ✓Patrick v. Ridge, 394 F.3d 311, 317 (5th Cir. 2004) ("vague" subjective reasons can support pretext where contradicted by objective performance record).

The January review is your pretext lever. "Ready for promotion" in writing, four months before being passed over for someone with less experience, on the basis of an unquantified "presence" judgment, is exactly the pattern Patrick says a jury can reasonably read as pretextual.

Recommended next step: pull the dispatch logs into evidence and request the comparator's full personnel file in pre-suit discovery prep. If Apex has nothing in writing on "leadership presence" before this promotion decision, that's a pretext win.

Open the Council. What's the Contrarian's dissent?
● Council Chairvia the five seats2s ago

Contrarian's dissent: the "ready for promotion" line is a phrase from a performance review, not a binding determination. Apex's counsel will argue that ready means qualified-to-be-considered, not selected. The Fifth Circuit has been willing to credit subjective hiring criteria when documented contemporaneously — and Apex may produce notes from the interview panel.

Mitigation: get those interview-panel notes in discovery before filing. If they don't exist or were drafted post-hoc, the pretext argument tightens. If they exist and substantively discuss "leadership presence" with specifics, the case is harder.

Majority (Researcher / Analyst / Strategist / Executor) holds prima facie + pretext story; Contrarian flags the interview-notes risk. Recommendation: proceed but front-load the interview-notes request.
