# Dear Chief Judge.
## A Modest Revision of Sullivan & Cromwell's Letter to the Court.

*Monday.*

The most important document filed in the Southern District of New York in the month of April was not a motion. It was an apology. Three pages, pinned to ECF on April 18th by partners at Sullivan & Cromwell, a firm that has been billing in twentieth-of-an-hour increments since approximately the McKinley administration, addressed to the Honorable Martin Glenn of the United States Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York, and devoted to the question of how exactly the firm's filings in *In re Prince Global Holdings Ltd.*, No. 26-10769 — the Chapter 15 ancillary case for the Prince Group, the Cambodian forced-labor cyber-scam empire that the United States Treasury sanctioned last October — came to be salted with citations to cases that do not exist.

I have a copy of the letter in front of me. I have read it three times. I have annotated it in pencil. In the margin next to the second paragraph, I have written *no.* In the margin next to the fourth paragraph, I have written *NO.* My assistant — who has been with me since the second Bush administration and has seen enough partner outbursts to qualify, in any sane jurisdiction, as a hostile witness — left the office at two and has not returned my calls.

What S&C produced, for the record, was a document of professional contrition that includes the following architectural elements: an opening acknowledgment that the firm "became aware" of the errors on *the ninth day after filing* — a temporal detail I will return to in a moment — a passive-voice description of the errors themselves (they "include" hallucinations, the way a buffet "includes" mayonnaise), a recitation of the firm's preexisting policies and training requirements (which the firm itself notes "were not followed"), a list of the remedial measures undertaken (re-review of all filings, additional training, etc.), and a closing apology to the Court for the inconvenience.

I want to draw your attention to the part of the letter that reads — and I am quoting exactly —

> *"Hallucinations are instances in which artificial intelligence tools fabricate case citations, misquote authorities, or generate non-existent legal sources."*

This is a definitional sentence. In a letter from Sullivan & Cromwell. To a federal judge. Explaining what a hallucination is. To Chief Judge Martin Glenn, who has been on the federal bench since 2007, who oversaw the General Motors bankruptcy, who has presided over more sophisticated artificial-intelligence-related malpractice than the partners at S&C who drafted this letter have had hot meals — as if the man required a glossary.

There is a paragraph later in the letter in which the firm explains that *"the Firm has comprehensive policies and training requirements governing the use of AI tools in legal work."*

There is then a sentence which clarifies that those policies *"were not followed in connection with the preparation of the Motion."*

There is then another sentence which clarifies that, *"in addition,"* the Firm's general policies and training requirements for the proper review of legal citations were *also not followed.*

The two sentences are placed next to each other. These are obviously not the words of the sin-eater Andrew Dietderich, whose name is on the signature block. They are the words of a drafter who has been asked to draft a document by a partner who is out of town, reviewed by a junior partner, approved by an ethics committee that is now wishing it had not been, proofed by a paralegal whose primary skill is producing a clean PDF, and signed by Mr. Dietderich, who apparently did not notice that the second sentence renders the first one a confession.

I want to be fair to my colleagues at S&C, whom I have known for only forty years, with whom I have opposed and co-counseled, whose offices I have sat in, whose partners I have respected in roughly the way one respects a structurally unsound bridge — for the engineering, not the trip across it.

So I have, on my own time, as a professional courtesy to a firm I admire in this same load-bearing way, prepared the letter they should have filed.

## A NOTE ON SCHEDULE A.

The actual Schedule A — the document S&C did file, beneath the apology — runs an additional two pages and corrects errors across *five separate declarations and filings.* Not one. Five. The corrections include:

— in the **Declaration of Andrew Chissick** (¶¶ 20, 33, 48, 50, 53, 60, 65): a missing footnote, a verb-tense correction, statutory citations that referred to the wrong sub-sections of the BVI Insolvency Act, BVI Orders citations that left out one of the orders entirely, and the phrase *"the The"* surviving final review at ¶ 60 — *"BCA § 245 ('For ~~the~~ The purposes of determining matters…')."* The phrase *"the The"* was reviewed, approved, proofed, signed, and filed.

— in the **Declaration of Paul Pretlove** (¶¶ 9, 21, 25, 27, 39, 42, 45, 46): a citation to *In re Three Arrows Capital, Ltd.* with the wrong reporter (*649 B.R. 143* corrected to *No. 24-10210 (MG)*), the wrong page number, the wrong year (*2023* corrected to *2024*); a UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights title cited under a title the UN OHCHR has never used (*"Online Scam Operations and Trafficking into Forced Criminality in Southeast Asia"* corrected to *"Seeking Human Rights-Based Solutions to Trafficking into Cyber-Scam Operations in South-East Asia"* — these are not the same title); an Amnesty International report whose subtitle has been silently altered from *"Forced Online Scam Labour in Cambodia"* to *"Slavery, Human Trafficking and Torture in Cambodia's Scamming Compounds"* — also, again, not the same title; and an *Id.* citation that, on inspection, refers to a paragraph in a different declaration.

— in the **Verified Petition** (¶ 28): a Westlaw star-page citation to *In re Fairfield Sentry* corrected from *\*10-16* to *\*7.* A magnitude error.

— in the **Motion for Joint Administration** (¶ 6): Federal Rule of Bankruptcy Procedure 1015(b) misquoted to refer to *"petitions"* where the Rule actually says *"cases."* Twice. This is not a hallucination. This is a partner at Sullivan & Cromwell who did not check the language of the rule he was about to invoke. The rule is one of the most-cited rules in the entire bankruptcy procedural code.

— in the **Motion for Entry of an Order Scheduling the Recognition Hearing** (¶¶ 6-7): a quotation from Bankruptcy Rule 2002(q)(1) that, in the original filing, ended with the phrase *"may imposes"* — a subject-verb agreement error of the kind we used to flag in second-year associates, and which the firm has, apparently, given up on flagging in named partners. A reference to *Section 1517(d)* of the Bankruptcy Code corrected to *Bankruptcy Rule 6004(h).* A confusion between a Code section and a Rule.

This is not Schedule A of a routine filing. This is a confession that the AI was permitted not only to invent citations the firm did not check, but to mangle citations the firm could have checked by typing *Bankruptcy Rule 1015(b)* into a search engine of any kind.

There is, in other words, a difference between *hallucinated* and *unread.* Schedule A documents both.

## A NOTE ON THE SIGNATURE.

The letter was signed by **Andrew G. Dietderich**.

I want to spend a moment on this signature, because it is, in its quiet way, the single most damning element of the document.

Mr. Dietderich is a Partner at Sullivan & Cromwell. He is also the named author of the firm's *Spotlight on Corporate Governance and Restructuring* (November 2021) — a piece of advisory writing published on the firm's own website, in which Mr. Dietderich was, at the time of publication, the firm's voice on the responsibilities of restructuring counsel, the centrality of corporate governance, and the ethical posture appropriate to the practice. The man who signed the apology for the firm's failure to follow its own ethics policies is the man whose name appears on the firm's commentary about ethics policies.

There is more.

Mr. Dietderich was also the partner of record at Sullivan & Cromwell during the FTX bankruptcy — a matter for which the firm billed the FTX estate approximately one hundred and eighty million dollars in fees between November 2022 and January 2024. On November 7, 2022, Mr. Dietderich personally told Voyager Digital — at the time a counterparty to FTX's proposed acquisition of it — that FTX's finances were *"rock solid"* and that questions raised about the deal were *"silliness."* He used the words *rock solid* and *silliness.* Four days later, FTX filed for Chapter 11. Mr. Bankman-Fried was eventually convicted of stealing eight billion dollars from FTX customers.

The court-appointed examiner, Robert Cleary — a former federal prosecutor and the man who put the Unabomber away — concluded that Sullivan & Cromwell had *"made false statements to outside parties"* but had not, at the time, *"known the statements were untrue."*

I would like you to read that sentence again.

*The firm made false statements to outside parties but did not know the statements were untrue.*

This is what professional negligence looks like in its mature form, after it has been worked over by the firm's own outside counsel and approved by the firm's own outside counsel's outside counsel. The statements were false. The firm made them. The firm did not know they were false *because the firm had not asked.* The examiner cleared the firm of complicity. The examiner did not — could not — clear the firm of the underlying behavior, which is the behavior of a counsel that confidently restates what its client has told it without applying the diligence the client is paying for.

This is the same partner. This is the same firm. This is the same letter, three and a half years later, just with a different machine producing the text the firm declined to read.

The pattern is consistent. When the firm is wrong, the firm did not know it was wrong. The firm regrets the inconvenience. The firm has implemented remedial measures. The firm is at the Amagansett house.

I am not, to be clear, making a legal argument against Andrew Dietderich. The man is an accomplished restructuring partner who has built one of the more profitable bankruptcy practices in the country. I am making an observation about the structural pattern, which is that the man whose name appears on the apology letter is the same man who *wrote the firm's article on the ethics this letter concedes were not followed*, which is the same man who told a counterparty FTX was *"rock solid"* four days before FTX wasn't, which is the same man who is now signing the explanation that the firm's training programs are excellent.

The signature is the joke. The signature is also the indictment.

## WHAT THE COURT CAUGHT.

What I have produced above is a parody. Sullivan & Cromwell will not file it. They will not file it because the partners who drafted the original letter are still partners and the firm's malpractice carrier is still its malpractice carrier and the structural incentive of every BigLaw apology letter is to say as little as possible while sounding as contrite as possible while admitting absolutely nothing that could be quoted back in a later proceeding. The form is well-worn. The form works. The form is the reason these letters all read like they were generated by the same AI tool whose output they are apologizing for, which — and I want to be very precise here — they may in fact have been.

What is interesting about the *Prince Global* matter, and what nobody at S&C will say in writing, is that **Judge Glenn caught it.** The Court caught it. Not the firm. Not the firm's policies. Not the firm's training. Not the firm's review protocols. The other side, and the bench, did the work the firm's $1,800-an-hour partner was supposed to do. The firm then drafted a letter explaining that its training programs are excellent. *(Bad.)*

There is an additional detail in the timeline. The Motion was filed on April 9th. The apology letter says the firm "became aware" of the errors "Thursday evening." Thursday was April 17th. *The firm became aware on the ninth day after filing.* The firm became aware after opposing counsel had reviewed it, after the bench had reviewed it, and after — one can fairly infer — opposing counsel had pointed at the errors with a degree of professional pleasure that I, having been opposing counsel many times, can attest is real. *Became aware* is not an active verb. *Became aware* is what one says when one has been told.

I represent clients who do this. I have litigated against opposing counsel who do this. I have, in moments I am not proud of, done versions of this. The technology has changed the speed and the surface area but the underlying behavior — *put your name on something you have not personally verified, and hope no one checks* — predates the LLM by approximately the entire history of the legal profession. The AI is not the problem. The AI is the velocity at which the problem now arrives.

If Sullivan & Cromwell wants the Court to take seriously its claim that it has remediated, the firm could begin by retaining outside counsel — not from the firm, not from a firm S&C does business with, not from a firm whose senior partners summer in Amagansett with S&C's senior partners — to perform a top-to-bottom audit of every AI-touched filing in the firm's bankruptcy docket for the past eighteen months.

The firm will not do this. The firm will not do this because the firm understands, with the clarity of an organization that has been in continuous operation since the Hayes administration, that an audit conducted in good faith would produce findings the firm cannot afford to be in writing. *(Bad.)*

The associate who drafted the motion is, I assume, no longer with us. The partner who signed it is, I assume, still with us. There is a reason for this. There has always been a reason for this. The reason has not changed. The technology has changed. The reason has not. And for that, the partner is internally grateful.

*First*, said the man in the play, *kill all the lawyers.*

He was not, as is commonly supposed, calling for our destruction. He was identifying the bottleneck.
