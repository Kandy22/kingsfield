# First, Kill All the Lawyers.
## On the Apology Letter Sullivan & Cromwell Filed, and the One They Should Have.

*Monday.*

The most important document filed in the Southern District of New York in the month of April was not a motion. It was an apology. Three pages, pinned to ECF on April 18th by partners at Sullivan & Cromwell, a firm that has been billing in twentieth-of-an-hour increments since approximately the McKinley administration, addressed to the Honorable Martin Glenn of the United States Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York, and devoted to the question of how exactly the firm's filings in *In re Prince Global Holdings Ltd.*, No. 26-10769, came to be salted with citations to cases that do not exist.

I have a copy of the letter in front of me. I have read it three times. I have annotated it in pencil. In the margin next to the second paragraph, I have written *no.* In the margin next to the fourth paragraph, I have written *NO.* My assistant — who has been with me since the second Bush administration and has seen enough partner outbursts to qualify, in any sane jurisdiction, as a hostile witness — left the office at two and has not returned my calls.

What S&C produced, for the record, was a document of professional contrition that includes the following architectural elements: an opening acknowledgment that the firm "became aware" of the errors, a passive-voice description of the errors themselves (they "include" hallucinations, the way a buffet "includes" mayonnaise), a recitation of the firm's preexisting policies and training requirements (which the firm itself notes "were not followed"), a list of the remedial measures undertaken (re-review of all filings, additional training, etc.), and a closing apology to the Court for the inconvenience.

I want to draw your attention to the part of the letter that reads — and I am quoting exactly — *"Hallucinations are instances in which artificial intelligence tools fabricate case citations, misquote authorities, or generate non-existent legal sources."*

This is a definitional sentence. In a letter from Sullivan & Cromwell. To a federal judge. Explaining what a hallucination is. To Chief Judge Martin Glenn — who has been on the federal bench since 2007, who oversaw the General Motors bankruptcy, who has presided over more sophisticated artificial-intelligence-related malpractice than the partners at S&C who drafted this letter have had hot meals — as if the man required a glossary.

There is a paragraph later in the letter in which the firm explains that "the Firm has comprehensive policies and training requirements governing the use of AI tools in legal work." There is then a sentence which clarifies that those policies "were not followed in connection with the preparation of the Motion." There is then another sentence which clarifies that, in addition, the firm's general policies and training requirements for the proper review of legal citations were also not followed. The two sentences are placed next to each other. The drafter of this letter — and I assume it was drafted by an associate, and reviewed by a partner, and approved by an ethics committee, and proofed by a paralegal, and signed by someone whose name is on the door — apparently did not notice that the second sentence rendered the first one a confession.

I want to be fair to my colleagues at S&C, whom I have known for forty years, with whom I have opposed and co-counseled, whose offices I have sat in, whose partners I have respected in roughly the way one respects a structurally unsound bridge — for the engineering, not the trip across it.

So I have, on my own time, as a professional courtesy to a firm I admire in this same load-bearing way, prepared the letter they should have filed.

>
> The Firm did not invent these cases. The Firm employed a technology that invented these cases. The Firm then failed to detect that the cases had been invented. The Firm then signed and filed the cases under the imprimatur of a Rule 11 obligation that the Firm has carried, in one form or another, since 1879. We acknowledge that this distinction — between inventing the cases and merely vouching for them — may be of greater interest to us than to Your Honor.
>
> By way of explanation, and not excuse:[^4]
>
> 1. The motion was drafted using an artificial intelligence tool that produces text indistinguishable, to a partner of this Firm, from the work of a competent third-year associate. The tool also produces, with the same confidence and in the same prose register, citations to cases that have never been decided. The Firm has been aware of this characteristic since approximately 2023.
>
> 2. The Firm maintains comprehensive policies and training requirements governing the use of AI tools in legal work. These policies were not followed.
>
> 3. The Firm also maintains comprehensive policies and training requirements governing the manual review of legal citations, which apply regardless of whether AI tools were involved. These policies were also not followed.
>
> 4. Upon a reasonable inquiry, we have determined that the policies which were not followed are, individually and in combination, the only structural protections against precisely the outcome that occurred. We are unable to offer Your Honor an explanation for why we did not follow them other than that we did not.
>
> The Firm has undertaken immediate remedial measures, including but not limited to:
>

>

>

> (d) the placement of a small handwritten card on the desk of every associate and partner in the bankruptcy group, which reads: *Did You Check The Cite.*
>

What I have produced above is a parody. Sullivan & Cromwell will not file it. They will not file it because the partners who drafted the original letter are still partners and the firm's malpractice carrier is still its malpractice carrier and the structural incentive of every BigLaw apology letter is to say as little as possible while sounding as contrite as possible while admitting absolutely nothing that could be quoted back in a later proceeding. The form is well-worn. The form works. The form is the reason these letters all read like they were generated by the same AI tool whose output they are apologizing for, which — and I want to be very precise here — they may in fact have been.

What is interesting about the Prince Global matter, and what nobody at S&C will say in writing, is that Judge Glenn caught it. The Court caught it. Not the firm. Not the firm's policies. Not the firm's training. Not the firm's review protocols. The other side, and the bench, did the work the firm's $1,800-an-hour partner was supposed to do. The firm then drafted a letter explaining that its training programs are excellent.

I represent clients who do this. I have litigated against opposing counsel who do this. I have, in moments I am not proud of, done versions of this. The technology has changed the speed and the surface area but the underlying behavior — *put your name on something you have not personally verified, and hope no one checks* — predates the LLM by approximately the entire history of the legal profession. The AI is not the problem. The AI is the velocity at which the problem now arrives.

If Sullivan & Cromwell wants the Court to take seriously its claim that it has remediated, the firm could begin by retaining outside counsel — not from the firm, not from a firm S&C does business with, not from a firm whose senior partners summer in Amagansett with S&C's senior partners — to perform a top-to-bottom audit of every AI-touched filing in the firm's bankruptcy docket for the past eighteen months. The firm will not do this. The firm will not do this because the firm understands, with the clarity of an organization that has been in continuous operation since the Hayes administration, that an audit conducted in good faith would produce findings the firm cannot afford to be in writing.

The associate who drafted the motion is, I assume, no longer with us. The partner who signed it is, I assume, still with us. There is a reason for this. There has always been a reason for this. The reason has not changed. The technology has changed. The reason has not.

*First*, said the man in the play, *kill all the lawyers.*

He was not, as is commonly supposed, calling for our destruction. He was identifying the bottleneck.
