## Post 1: The Junior Associate Who Cited a Case That Did Not Exist (A Eulogy)

*Monday.*

His name was — well, his name isn't relevant. He was a third-year who had been with us for eleven months. Good school, acceptable grades, one of those eager Midwestern types who shows up early and sends emails at 11 PM to demonstrate effort, as if effort were the thing I was paying for and not results.

He submitted a research memo on Friday afternoon. Forty-three pages. Thoroughly Bluebooked. Confident. He had found, he told me in the cover note, "compelling circuit-level authority" for our position.

I read the memo on Saturday morning.

I called the partner covering that matter and told him to read it before he did anything else.

Then I called the associate.

"The Feldstein case," I said. "Tell me about it."

Silence.

"*Feldstein v. Advanced Biomedics*," I continued. "United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit. 2021. The case you cite four times, including for the proposition that a limitation-of-liability clause is unenforceable against a sophisticated commercial party when the drafting party had superior information. You cite it. Twice in the body. Once in the footnote. Once more in the conclusion as a kicker."

More silence.

"I cannot find it," I said. "I have checked CourtListener. I have checked Westlaw. I have checked the Second Circuit's own opinion archive going back to 1994. *Feldstein v. Advanced Biomedics* does not exist. It has never existed. It is, as best I can determine, a hallucination produced by the artificial intelligence product you were apparently using to do what I was paying you to do."

He told me he had "verified" it.

I asked him what "verified" meant.

He said he had "asked the AI to confirm it."

I want to pause here and let that sink in. He asked the AI to confirm whether the AI had invented a case. He received the answer "yes, this is a real case." He wrote it into the memo. He sent it to a named partner at a firm that bills $1,100 an hour and considers that a bargain.

In *Mata v. Avianca*, 22 Civ. 1461 (S.D.N.Y. 2023), Judge Castel sanctioned two attorneys for filing a brief containing six cases that ChatGPT had fabricated wholesale. The cases had invented parties, invented dockets, invented holdings. The attorneys, when asked to produce the cases, produced more fabrications. The AI helpfully generated fake quotes from fake judges to support the fake opinions.

Judge Castel was not amused. He used the phrase "bogus judicial decisions" in his opinion. He fined the attorneys $5,000 apiece. He ordered them to serve copies of his sanctions order on the parties. He wrote, with the specific cruelty of a man who has been on the bench long enough to stop pretending surprise: "There is nothing in the rules of conduct to address the fact that an attorney who follows AI's advice in filing arguments may be adopting the AI's errors as his own."

Nothing in the rules. Except, of course, Rule 11. And Rule 3.1. And a lawyer's basic professional obligation to know what he is putting in front of a court before he puts it there.

My associate is no longer with us.

This is not, primarily, a story about artificial intelligence. The AI did what AI does — it produced confident, well-formatted, plausible-sounding output that happened to be completely false. That is a known characteristic of these systems. It has been a known characteristic since before this associate graduated from law school. He knew about *Mata v. Avianca*. Everyone knows about *Mata v. Avianca*. It is the *Palsgraf* of AI legal malpractice and has been discussed in every continuing education seminar for the past two years.

This is a story about a third-year who decided that the AI confirming its own output was sufficient verification. It is a story about a lawyer who treated a large language model the way a lazy associate treats a headnote — as a shortcut around reading the actual case.

The difference is that a headnote at least points to something real.

I've now implemented a verification protocol for every matter in this office. Every cited authority passes four gates before it appears in any document: existence, quote accuracy, currency, jurisdiction fit. The gates are not optional. They are not annoying bureaucratic formalities. They are the difference between being a lawyer and being someone who plays one on a computer.

The Midwest is lovely this time of year. I'm sure he'll find something to do there.

## Post 2: On the Wellness App

*Tuesday.*

My assistant forwarded me a note from an associate asking whether the firm would cover the cost of a "mental wellness app subscription."

Thirty-two dollars a month.

I should note that I am aware "mental wellness" is a recognized area of concern in the legal profession. I have read the statistics. I have attended the seminars. I understand that associate attrition is expensive, that burnout is real, that the psychological cost of billing two thousand hours a year is non-trivial. I am not a monster. I own two mental health books that I have not read but whose spines are visible from my desk, which I consider a gesture.

I sent the note back with a single line: *"The firm does not cover wellness apps. The firm is a law firm. The correct response to being unwell is to bill through it."*

She came to my office. That was the first mistake.

She said — and I want to get this right because it was remarkable — she said that she was "struggling with the always-on culture" and that she "needed to protect her boundaries."

I asked her what a boundary was, in this context.

She said it was "a limit on what she was willing to give to the job."

I looked at her for a moment. Then I asked her to close my door on her way out, which she did, and then I called HR and asked them to put a note in her file.

Here is what I want to explain, and I want to explain it calmly, because I think it gets lost in what passes for discourse about BigLaw culture: I did not choose this life. The life was presented to me as a series of requirements, and I met them. When I was a fifth-year, my senior partner made me review the same exhibit forty-three times because he didn't like the font. I reviewed the exhibit forty-three times. I did not ask him to honor my boundaries. I did not download an app. I got better at anticipating the fonts he preferred.

You endure the arbitrary until the arbitrary is yours to dispense. That is the entire value proposition. The people at the top did not get there by protecting themselves from the job. They got there by letting the job do what it wanted to them until there was nothing left for it to take, and then discovering that on the other side of that was partnership.

The associate who sent me that email will leave in six months. She'll go in-house somewhere and work forty hours a week and wonder why she feels like she's missing something. What she's missing is the particular satisfaction that comes from being so competent at something difficult that it doesn't matter anymore whether you enjoy it.

Thirty-two dollars a month.

I can spend thirty-two dollars on a single olive in the right restaurant. I'm not covering your app.

## Post 3: Royalties Accounting, Or: How the Record Label Stole Your Money Legally

*Wednesday.*

A client came in last week — an artist, three albums for a major label, reasonably well-known, the kind of name you'd recognize if you were the right age. He'd been with the label for twelve years. He came in because he'd started doing his own math and the numbers weren't adding up.

I'll spare you the specific details because I have a confidentiality clause and because the specific details would simply cause you pain. Instead I'll explain the architecture, because the architecture is the same across the industry, has been the same across the industry since approximately 1972, and represents one of the more elegant systems of legal theft ever constructed by human beings.

Here is how the recording industry accounts for royalties.

First, the base. Your royalty is calculated on the "published price to dealers," not the retail price, not what the consumer pays, not what the streaming service pays the label. The PPTD. This number is set by the label. It does not appear anywhere on anything the consumer sees. It is, in practice, whatever the label says it is.

Second, the reductions. Your royalty rate — let's say fifteen percent, which sounds reasonable — is applied after a series of packaging deductions. For a CD, historically, labels deducted twenty-five percent for "packaging" before calculating your rate. On a streaming file. There is no packaging on a streaming file. There is no physical object. There is a sequence of ones and zeros delivered over an internet connection that you helped pay to build. The packaging deduction applies anyway, because the contract says it does, and the contract was drafted by the label's attorneys, and you signed it at age twenty-four.

Third, the reserves. Labels are permitted, under standard agreements, to withhold a "reasonable reserve" against returns. Returns. Of streaming plays. Streaming plays cannot be returned. The file has been delivered. The listener heard it. It is physically impossible for them to un-hear it and send it back. The reserve exists because it existed when vinyl records could be returned by retailers, and it has never been removed from the contract because it represents money the label keeps.

Fourth, the audit rights. You may audit the label's accounting once every two years, at your own expense, with sixty days' notice, for royalty periods not older than four years. The audit must be conducted by a CPA the label approves. You pay for the audit. If the audit finds an error of less than ten percent, you pay the label's legal fees for disputing the accounting.

Less than ten percent.

Do you understand what that means? If the label is stealing from you and stealing from you competently — if they are stealing from you at a rate of nine point nine percent — you pay for the privilege of discovering it.

My client had been underpaid by approximately $340,000 over twelve years. We found it in three weeks once we had the data. It wasn't hidden with any particular sophistication. It was just there, in the accounting, in the perfectly legal application of contract terms that most artists never read and most lawyers never understand because they require forensic accounting expertise that most entertainment lawyers don't have.

The label offered to "correct the accounting going forward."

Going forward.

I told them that was a generous offer and that I would relay it to my client before explaining to a jury what "correcting the accounting going forward" means when preceded by $340,000 in what I was prepared to characterize, with exhibits, as intentional underpayment.

We settled. My client is satisfied. The label's accounting practices remain exactly what they were.

I'll be back next year with a different client. Same architecture, different numbers.

## Post 5: The Billable Hour Is Not the Problem. You Are the Problem.

*Friday.*

There is a genre of article that gets written approximately four times a year in which a law professor or a legal technology journalist explains that the billable hour is broken, that it creates perverse incentives, that it rewards time spent rather than value delivered, and that if we just moved to alternative fee arrangements the legal industry would transform into a place of pure merit and efficient service and everyone would be happier.

I want to explain why this is wrong.

Not wrong as in "there are counterarguments." Wrong as in: this is a description of a different problem wearing the billable hour's clothes.

The billable hour does create perverse incentives. This is true. A lawyer who bills by the hour has a structural reason to be slower rather than faster, more thorough rather than targeted, to revisit rather than conclude. These incentives exist. I am aware of them because I have spent forty years navigating them on behalf of clients who were paying attention.

But here is what the people who hate the billable hour never say: the alternative is that you pay for the outcome.

You would like to pay for the outcome. You believe this is better. You believe this because you have not yet had an outcome that was wrong.

In a fixed-fee engagement, I give you a price. The price is based on my estimate of how long the work will take and how much risk I'm carrying. If the work goes badly — if the other side finds something we didn't anticipate, if the regulation changes, if your former CFO turns out to have been doing something that becomes relevant in discovery — I absorb that cost or I bill you for the variance and we have a disagreement. If the work goes quickly and cleanly, I keep the margin.

The result of this arrangement is not that lawyers work faster. The result is that lawyers price the risk into the upfront number, which means the client pays for the worst-case scenario whether or not it materializes, and the lawyer is incentivized to do the minimum work consistent with an acceptable outcome rather than the thorough work that would have found the problem before the deposition.

The billable hour, for all its flaws, is transparent. You see the time. You see the rate. You can question the time. You can negotiate the rate. You know exactly what you paid and you can evaluate whether you got what you paid for.

The alternative fee arrangement is opaque by design. The price is a black box. The work product is a black box. You have no way of knowing whether your attorney spent twenty hours or two on the matter, whether they found everything or found enough. You are buying a result without visibility into how it was produced.

I do not argue that the billable hour is perfect. I argue that the people who want to abolish it have not thought carefully about what they want in its place.

What they want, if you press them, is a world where legal services are cheaper. That is a reasonable thing to want. Legal services are expensive. They are expensive because lawyers are expensive because law school is expensive because the credential required to practice law requires three years of post-graduate education that is largely disconnected from the actual practice of law — but that is a different problem.

The billable hour is not what makes legal services expensive. What makes legal services expensive is that you need a lawyer and lawyers have a monopoly on being lawyers.

Fix that, and we can talk about fee structures.

Until then: the hour rates are on my website, the retainer is non-negotiable, and if you'd like to discuss the value I've delivered over forty years of practice, I have receipts.
