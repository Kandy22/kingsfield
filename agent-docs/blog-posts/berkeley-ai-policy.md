# Smart. Not Stupid.
## Berkeley Law's AI Policy, or: The Sine Qua Non of Missing the Point

*Monday.*

UC Berkeley School of Law has released its official AI policy for Summer 2026, and I want to read you something from it.

*"Thinking remains the sine qua non of good lawyering."*

They wrote this. In a policy document. About artificial intelligence. The sine qua non. They used Latin to explain that thinking is important. At a law school. Which is presumably where people go to learn to think.

Let me walk you through what Berkeley has decided their students may not do: brainstorm, outline, draft, revise, edit, or translate. Using AI. For credit. They have enumerated these things in bullet points, as if the students needed confirmation that "asking an AI to compose a paragraph" counts as prohibited drafting and not some exciting new category of academic integrity violation that nobody had considered.

They have also decided — and this is my favorite part — that AI *can* be used for research, but only to identify sources. You can use the tool to find the case. You may not use the tool to read the case, summarize the case, think about the case, or do anything with the case other than note that it exists.

Here is what is actually happening. The legal industry is undergoing the most significant structural disruption in a century. Law firms are billing clients for AI-generated work product while simultaneously telling associates that using AI is cheating. Clients are building in-house AI legal teams that can do in four hours what a first-year associate would do in forty. The Am Law 100 just reported that associate headcount is down 12% while revenue per lawyer is up 18%. The economics of legal services are being restructured around you in real time.

And Berkeley Law's response — their considered, faculty-approved, effective-Summer-2026 response — is that you may not ask an AI to find your repetitive passages.

This is like a driving school in 1910 forbidding students from asking someone else to start the car, on the grounds that cranking the engine builds character.

The students who figure out how to use these tools rigorously — how to verify the output, interrogate the reasoning, catch the hallucinations, and deploy AI where it helps without letting it replace where thinking is irreplaceable — those students will eat the ones who learned legal writing the old-fashioned way for breakfast. Not because the old-fashioned skills don't matter. Because those skills plus the tools beat those skills alone, every time, by a factor that is growing quarterly.

Berkeley has decided the answer to this is a prohibition.

The firms interviewing their graduates are using the tools right now. Today. On the matters those students will work on when they graduate. The gap between what is being taught and what the practice actually requires is not a gap — it's a canyon, and Berkeley just put up a sign on their side that says *DO NOT CROSS, THINKING REQUIRED.*

Good luck to the Class of 2028.

*They're going to need the Latin.*
